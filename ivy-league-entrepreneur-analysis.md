# Ivy League Tech/Engineering Graduates: Entrepreneurship & Success Rates

## A Data-Driven Estimate

This analysis attempts to estimate: (1) what percentage of Ivy League tech/engineering graduates become entrepreneurs, and (2) of those, what percentage "make it big."

---

## Part 1: What % of Ivy League Tech/Engineering Grads Become Entrepreneurs?

### The Data Points

| Data Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| Harvard Gazette (2015) Alumni Survey | **39%** of all Harvard alumni have founded a for-profit or nonprofit venture (146,000+ companies across 150+ countries) |
| Harvard Business School Class of 2025 | **17%** of HBS MBA grads planned to start their own company (up from 8% in 2021) |
| Kauffman Foundation (2008) | **92%** of U.S.-born tech founders hold bachelor's degrees; ~50% of those degrees are in STEM fields |
| BLS National Baseline | **~10%** of the total U.S. workforce is self-employed |
| Kauffman Foundation | Only **~8%** of U.S.-born tech company founders came from Ivy League schools |

### Estimating the Entrepreneurship Rate

**Key context:** The Ivy League collectively graduates ~65,000 undergrads and produces ~16,000+ bachelor's degrees per year across all 8 schools. Engineering/CS/STEM makes up roughly **20-30%** of degrees depending on the school (e.g., Columbia's engineering school is ~25% of undergrads; Cornell's engineering college is larger at ~20% of total enrollment).

**Best estimate: ~15-25% of Ivy League tech/engineering grads become entrepreneurs at some point in their careers.**

How we get there:
- The Harvard alumni survey (39% founding a venture) is the broadest data point, but it includes *all* Harvard alumni (including MBA/law/med), counts nonprofit ventures, and spans entire careers. It also skews high because Harvard's entrepreneurial culture is among the strongest.
- Engineering/STEM grads likely have a *higher* rate of tech entrepreneurship than the average Ivy grad, since the Kauffman data shows ~50% of tech founders have STEM degrees.
- The national self-employment rate is ~10%, but this undercounts entrepreneurial attempts (many founders return to employment after a venture fails or exits).
- Adjusting the Harvard 39% figure downward for the "strict" definition of tech entrepreneurship (not counting small consulting shops, nonprofits, etc.) lands us in the **15-25%** range for STEM/engineering grads who attempt a serious startup at some point in their career.

For comparison, the general population rate of starting a business is roughly **10-13%** over a lifetime (per Census/BLS data), so Ivy League STEM grads are roughly **1.5-2x** more likely to try entrepreneurship.

## Part 2: Of Those Entrepreneurs, What % "Make It Big"?

### Defining "Making It Big"

We define three tiers:

| Tier | Definition | Threshold |
|---|---|---|
| **Unicorn** | $1B+ valuation | The top of the top |
| **Major Success** | $100M+ exit or sustained revenue | Significant wealth creation |
| **Funded & Growing** | Raised institutional VC funding | On the path, but outcome uncertain |

### The Data Points

| Data Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| SignalFire Unicorn DNA Report (2024) | All 8 Ivy League schools combined produced **13.8%** of ~2,000 unicorn founders (from ~800 U.S. unicorns founded 2010-2024). Top 6 engineering schools (Stanford, MIT, Berkeley, GT, CMU, Caltech) produced **16.3%** |
| Stanford/Strebulaev Unicorn Study | Per capita, Ivy League grads are **~1.0-1.6x** average likelihood of founding a unicorn (Stanford is 1.6x; some Ivy schools are below average on per-capita basis) |
| PitchBook 2025 Rankings | Harvard produced **3,842** graduate-level founders; Stanford produced **4,287**; these founders raised $222B and $242B respectively over the past decade |
| Crunchbase 2025 | ~25% of all U.S. seed/pre-seed investment goes to Stanford/Harvard/MIT alumni |
| Kauffman Foundation | Ivy League tech founders' companies averaged **$6.7M revenue** and **55 employees** — higher than the overall average |
| General VC Data (HBS/Ghosh) | **~75%** of VC-backed startups don't return cash to investors |
| General Startup Data | Only **~1%** of all startups reach unicorn status; **~10%** survive long-term |

### Estimating the "Make It Big" Rate

**Starting assumptions (per 1,000 Ivy League engineering/tech grads):**

```
1,000 Ivy League tech/engineering grads
  │
  ├── ~200 attempt entrepreneurship (20%)
  │     │
  │     ├── ~60 raise institutional VC funding (30% of attempts)
  │     │     │
  │     │     ├── ~15 achieve meaningful exit or $100M+ outcome (25% of funded)
  │     │     │     │
  │     │     │     └── ~2-3 reach unicorn status ($1B+)
  │     │     │
  │     │     └── ~45 fail to return capital to investors
  │     │
  │     └── ~140 bootstrap, fail early, or pivot to employment
  │
  └── ~800 pursue traditional careers (engineering, finance, consulting, etc.)
```

### Summary Estimates

| Outcome | % of All Ivy Tech/Eng Grads | % of Those Who Try Entrepreneurship |
|---|---|---|
| Attempt entrepreneurship | **~20%** | 100% |
| Raise VC funding | **~6%** | ~30% |
| Major success ($100M+ outcome) | **~1.5%** | ~7-8% |
| Unicorn ($1B+ valuation) | **~0.2-0.3%** | ~1-1.5% |

### How This Compares to the General Population

| Metric | Ivy League Tech/Eng Grads | General Population |
|---|---|---|
| Entrepreneurship attempt rate | ~20% | ~10-13% |
| Of entrepreneurs, raise VC | ~30% | ~1-2% |
| Of entrepreneurs, major success | ~7-8% | ~1-2% |
| Of entrepreneurs, unicorn | ~1-1.5% | ~0.001-0.01% |

The **VC access gap** is the single largest differentiator. Crunchbase data shows ~25% of all early-stage U.S. VC goes to just three schools (Stanford, Harvard, MIT). The Ivy League network effect on funding access is enormous — Ivy League entrepreneurs are roughly **15-30x** more likely to raise VC than the general population of entrepreneurs.

However, *conditional on raising VC*, the success rates converge somewhat. The ~75% VC-backed failure rate (per Harvard's Ghosh) applies broadly regardless of school.

## Part 3: A More Practical Definition — Earning $1M+/Year From Your Equity

The unicorn/$100M exit framing misses the broader population of entrepreneurs who build very successful, profitable businesses. A more practical "made it big" threshold: **earning $1M+ annually from your ownership stake** (e.g., owning 33% of a company generating $3M in annual profit).

### What Kind of Company Gets You There?

| Your Equity Stake | Required Company Annual Profit | Implied Revenue (at 20-30% margin) |
|---|---|---|
| 100% (bootstrapped, no co-founder) | $1M | $3-5M |
| 50% (bootstrapped, one co-founder) | $2M | $7-10M |
| 33% (two co-founders, no dilution) | $3M | $10-15M |
| 25% (post-Series A typical) | $4M | $13-20M |
| 15-20% (post-Series B typical) | $5-7M | $17-35M |

### Revenue Milestone Data

| Milestone | % of All Businesses That Reach It | % of Tech Startups That Reach It |
|---|---|---|
| $1M revenue | ~9% (JPMorgan Chase Institute) | ~4% (industry data) |
| $3M revenue | ~3-4% (estimated) | ~2% |
| $10M revenue | **<1%** (Census/SBA data) | ~0.4% of SaaS startups |
| $10M+ revenue | Only ~0.4% of SaaS companies (ChartMogul) | — |

### Founder Equity Dilution Reality (Carta 2025 Data)

| Stage | Median Founding Team Ownership |
|---|---|
| Pre-funding | ~100% |
| Post-Seed | **56%** |
| Post-Series A | **36%** |
| Post-Series B | **23%** |
| Post-Series C | **15-20%** |

This means a VC-backed founder at Series B who owns ~23% of the company needs the business to generate **~$4.3M in annual profit** for them to clear $1M/year — implying roughly **$15-20M in revenue** at typical tech margins.

### Two Paths to $1M/Year: VC-Backed vs. Bootstrapped

**Path A: VC-Backed (of 1,000 Ivy tech/eng grads)**

```
200 attempt entrepreneurship
 └── 60 raise VC
      └── Median ownership drops to ~25-35% by growth stage
           └── Need company profit of $3-4M+ → revenue of $10-20M
                └── ~10-15% of VC-backed startups reach $10M+ revenue
                     └── ~6-9 founders clear the $1M/year threshold
```

**Path B: Bootstrapped (of the same 1,000)**

```
200 attempt entrepreneurship
 └── 140 bootstrap or don't raise VC
      └── Retain 50-100% ownership
           └── Need company profit of $1-2M → revenue of $3-7M
                └── ~5% of bootstrapped tech businesses reach $3M+ revenue
                     └── ~7 founders clear the $1M/year threshold
```

### Combined Estimate: $1M+/Year From Equity

| Metric | Estimate |
|---|---|
| Of Ivy tech/eng entrepreneurs who try | **~5-10%** reach $1M+/year from equity |
| Of all Ivy tech/eng grads | **~1-2%** (roughly 1 in 50-100) |
| General population entrepreneur equivalent | **~1-2%** of entrepreneurs, or ~0.1-0.2% of all workers |

**The Ivy League advantage at this tier is roughly 5-10x the general population rate.**

### Why This Tier Is More Interesting Than Unicorns

- **It's 5-10x more common than unicorn outcomes** among Ivy entrepreneurs
- **It captures bootstrapped success** — many of the wealthiest small-business owners never raise a dollar of VC and never appear in Crunchbase or PitchBook data
- **It's achievable on multiple attempts** — the 30% success rate for serial entrepreneurs (Kauffman) means many founders hit this on their 2nd or 3rd company
- **It includes "boring" but profitable businesses** — B2B SaaS, dev tools, consulting-turned-product, e-commerce — that never make TechCrunch but make founders rich
- **Ivy League networks matter here too** — not just for VC access, but for landing enterprise contracts, recruiting talent, and getting warm intros to customers

## Key Takeaways

1. **~1 in 5** Ivy League tech/engineering grads will attempt entrepreneurship over their career — about 1.5-2x the national average.

2. **~1 in 10-20** of those who try will earn **$1M+/year from their equity** — the "practically rich" outcome. That's roughly **1-2% of all Ivy League tech/eng grads**, or about 1 in 50-100.

3. **~1 in 13** of those who try will achieve a major outcome ($100M+ exit). That's roughly **1.5% of all Ivy League tech/engineering graduates** — or about 1 in 65.

4. **~1 in 70-100** Ivy League tech entrepreneur attempts result in a unicorn ($1B+). That's roughly **0.2-0.3% of all Ivy League tech grads** — or about 1 in 400.

5. **The biggest advantage isn't higher success rates — it's access to capital.** Ivy League grads are ~15-30x more likely to raise VC than the general population. Once funded, their success/failure rates are similar to all VC-backed founders.

6. **Bootstrapping is an underrated path.** A founder retaining 50-100% of a $3-5M revenue business can out-earn many VC-backed founders who own 15-25% of a $20M revenue company — without the dilution, board pressure, or growth-at-all-costs mandate.

7. **Engineering schools are outpacing the Ivy League.** SignalFire's data shows the top 6 engineering schools (Stanford, MIT, Berkeley, etc.) produce more unicorn founders (16.3%) than the entire 8-school Ivy League (13.8%).

## Sources & Methodology Notes

This analysis triangulates across multiple datasets with different methodologies, time periods, and definitions. The estimates above are best-effort approximations, not precise measurements. Key limitations:

- "Entrepreneurship" definitions vary (Harvard's 39% includes nonprofit founding)
- Survivorship bias in alumni surveys (successful founders are more likely to respond)
- VC-backed startups are a small fraction of all entrepreneurship
- Engineering/CS/STEM is not cleanly separated in most datasets
- Time period matters: entrepreneurship rates have increased significantly since 2010
